Summary

Our dataset has 29,965 credit card clients from Taiwan, and $22.13\%$ of them default. If you just predict "nobody defaults", you get $77.87\%$ accuracy but catch zero defaulters. So accuracy is not a useful metric for this problem.

The two mistakes also don't cost the same. Missing a defaulter ($c_{FN}$) means the bank loses the unpaid balance, while a false alarm ($c_{FP}$) only costs some extra checking. If the model's probabilities are calibrated, the threshold with the lowest expected cost is:

$$t^* = \frac{c_{FP}}{c_{FP} + c_{FN}} = \frac{1}{1 + r}, \quad \text{where } r = \frac{c_{FN}}{c_{FP}}$$

So if a missed defaulter costs $5\times$ as much as a false alarm ($r = 5$), we should flag a client when $p \ge$ $\frac{1}{1 + 5} \approx 0.167$, not 0.5. On our test set, HistGradientBoosting costs $0.565$ per client at $t^* = 0.167$ versus $0.748$ at the usual $0.50$ threshold, because $0.50$ catches only 36% of defaulters instead of 72%.

Section 01

1. Our Question and What One Row Means

What one row means

Each row is one credit card client of a Taiwanese bank. It has their credit limit and some demographics, plus their repayment status, bill amount and payment amount for each month from April to September 2005. The target is whether the client defaulted on their payment in October 2005.

Research question (from our proposal)

"Using a client's repayment status, bill amounts, payment amounts and demographics from April to September 2005, can we predict whether they default next month, and which decision threshold minimizes expected cost when a missed defaulter costs more than a false alarm? Are the predicted probabilities calibrated, and how do regularized logistic regression, an SVM, random forest and boosting compare, including how ridge and lasso treat the six monthly bill-amount columns, which I expect to be strongly correlated?"

What we are not claiming

  • No causation: A feature that predicts default doesn't cause it. For example, a high credit limit going with fewer defaults doesn't mean raising limits would prevent defaults.
  • One time and place: The data is from Taiwan in 2005, during a period with a lot of card debt problems. The results may not hold for other countries or years.
  • Not a lending tool: The model predicts missed payments for existing card holders. It is not meant for deciding who gets a card.
  • Leaving out sex and marital status isn't enough for fairness: Other features can be correlated with them, so we also compare error rates across groups (Section 7).

Problems we found in the data and what we did

  • 35 duplicate rows: Every row has a different ID, so at first it looks like 30,000 different clients. But when we ignored the ID and compared the other 23 columns, 35 rows were exact copies. We removed them before splitting, so the same client can't end up in both train and test.
  • Odd column name: In the raw file, September's repayment status is called PAY_0 and the next one is PAY_2 (there is no PAY_1). We renamed PAY_0 to PAY_1 so it matches BILL_AMT1 and PAY_AMT1.
  • Category codes that aren't in the documentation:
    • EDUCATION should be 1 to 4, but 345 rows have 0, 5 or 6. We put them in 4 ('Others').
    • MARRIAGE should be 1 to 3, but 54 rows have 0. We put them in 3 ('Others').
  • Odd and extreme money values: We counted these but didn't remove them, since they look like real situations rather than errors (check_bill_values in src/data_loader.py):
    • 1,930 clients have a negative bill in some month (probably overpaid). Their default rate is 16.5%.
    • 3,931 clients went over their credit limit in some month (343 by more than 1.5×). Their default rate is 30.1%.
    • 836 clients have no bill at all in the six months.
    • Bills and payments are very skewed (largest bill 1,664,089 NT$). A signed log transform before Ridge improved its CV log-loss (0.4659 → 0.4546) but not its PR-AUC (0.504 vs. 0.503), and trees don't care about it, so we kept the pipeline as it was.
  • Very correlated bill columns: The bill amounts of neighbouring months are almost the same (correlation up to 0.955, VIF up to 17.08). Section 8b shows what this does to the linear models.
Section 03

3. Preprocessing and Why

What we did to the data before training, and why:

1. Scaling the numeric columns

Why: The columns are on very different scales: LIMIT_BAL and the bill amounts go above 500,000 NT$, while AGE is 20-70 and PAY is -2 to 8. Ridge, Lasso and the SVM all depend on scale, so without scaling the penalty (and the SVM distances) would treat the columns very unequally.

2. One-hot encoding education

Why: EDUCATION is a category, not a number (going from 1 to 2 isn't the same "step" as 3 to 4). We use OneHotEncoder(drop='first'). Dropping one column avoids the dummy variable trap for plain logistic regression, which has no penalty.

3. Leaving out sex and marital status

Why: We didn't want the model to use sex or marital status directly, so SEX and MARRIAGE are not in $X$. We keep them in a separate table only to check error rates per group afterwards.

4. Avoiding leakage with a Pipeline

Why: If you scale the whole dataset before cross-validation, the mean and standard deviation already include the validation (or test) rows, which is a small leak. We put the scaler and encoder inside a scikit-learn Pipeline, so they are only fit on the training folds each time.

4. The Models We Compared

We picked models from different families so we could compare simple vs. flexible models, see how the linear ones deal with the correlated bill columns, and check calibration:

Model Name Role How it works Why we included it
Majority Class Baseline Predicts "no default" for everyone. Shows why accuracy misleads: 77.87% accuracy, 0% recall and the highest cost of any model.
Flag Nobody / Flag Everybody Cost baselines Flag no one, or flag every client. A useful model has to be cheaper than both. They break even at $r = 3.52$.
Plain Logistic Regression Linear reference Ordinary logistic regression, no penalty. Simple, easy to interpret, and gives probabilities.
Ridge Logistic ($L_2$) Linear + L2 penalty Adds a penalty on the squared weights ($\lambda \sum w_j^2$). Shrinks all weights toward zero without removing any, which should make the weights of correlated bill months less extreme.
Lasso Logistic ($L_1$) Linear + L1 penalty Adds a penalty on the absolute weights ($\lambda \sum |w_j|$). Can set weights exactly to zero. Tests whether some of the six correlated bill months can be dropped.
Elastic-Net Logistic Linear + both A mix of the L1 and L2 penalties. Mix of the two penalties: some zeroing like Lasso, some shrinkage like Ridge.
RBF-Kernel SVM Non-linear Non-linear decision boundary through the RBF kernel; Platt scaling turns scores into probabilities. To see if a non-linear boundary helps. Platt scaling fits a sigmoid on the SVM scores to get probabilities.
Random Forest Bagging Averages 150 decision trees, each trained on a bootstrap sample with random feature subsets. Averaging many trees reduces variance, and trees aren't bothered much by the huge bill values.
HistGradientBoosting Boosting Adds trees one at a time, each one fitting the errors (gradients of log-loss) of the ones before. Each new tree corrects the errors of the previous ones, which reduces bias. Best model in our comparison.
Section 05

5. How We Evaluated

The split

  • 80/20 stratified split: Done once with random_state=42: 23,972 training clients and 5,993 test clients, both with a 22.13% default rate.
  • Test set used once: We didn't use the test set to choose features, hyperparameters or thresholds. Thresholds come from the formula $t^* = 1/(1+r)$; we checked on out-of-fold training predictions that the cost-minimising threshold is the same in practice (0.17 vs. 0.167 at $r=5$, see Section 9).
  • 5-fold CV for tuning: All hyperparameters were chosen with stratified 5-fold CV on the training set. We picked the setting with the lowest log-loss, because the cost threshold only works if the probabilities are good.

Why these metrics

  • PR-AUC: Accuracy and ROC-AUC look good just because there are so many non-defaulters. PR-AUC focuses on how well we find the defaulters.
  • Log-loss and Brier score: Measure how good the probabilities are, and punish confident wrong predictions.
  • ECE with equal-size bins: Measures calibration. Most predictions are low, so we use bins with the same number of clients instead of equal-width bins, otherwise the top bins would be almost empty.
  • Expected Cost: Cost per client $= \frac{FN \cdot r + FP}{N}$, in units of $c_{FP}$.
  • Bootstrap confidence intervals: We resample the test set 1,000 times to get 95% intervals for each metric (no refitting).

The Linear Models and the Six Bill Columns

All four linear models get almost the same test PR-AUC (about 0.503), so regularization hardly changes the predictions. It does change the weights though. In every linear model, BILL_AMT1 gets a big negative weight and BILL_AMT2 a big positive one. Since the two columns are almost the same, the model is really using the change in balance from August to September. Section 09

9. Are the Probabilities Calibrated?

Reliability curves (equal-size bins)

We use bins with the same number of clients, because most clients get a low predicted probability.

Why calibration matters here

The formula $t^* = \frac{1}{1 + r}$ only works if the probabilities mean what they say: out of clients with $\hat{p} = 0.3$, about 30% should actually default.

  • HistGradientBoosting: Well calibrated ($\text{ECE} = 0.0114$, $\text{Brier} = 0.1360$); its reliability curve stays close to the diagonal.
  • RBF-SVM with Platt Scaling: The sigmoid fitted on SVM scores gives reasonable probabilities ($\text{ECE} = 0.0290$), but worse than the tree ensembles.
  • Class weights vs. moving the threshold: We also tried training with class_weight='balanced' (checked with 5-fold out-of-fold predictions on the training set). For HistGradientBoosting the average predicted probability jumped from 0.22 to 0.42, even though the real default rate is 0.22, and ECE went from 0.006 to 0.196, so the probabilities were no longer calibrated. At $r=5$ the normal model with $t^*=0.167$ cost 0.544 per client, while the weighted model cost 0.566 at threshold 0.5 (and 0.704 at 0.167). For Ridge it went the other way: the weighted model at 0.5 was cheaper (0.620 vs. 0.694), but its ECE was 0.235. We kept the normal models because our best model does better that way and because calibrated probabilities let us use the formula for any $r$.
  • Threshold check: On out-of-fold predictions of HGB on the training set, the cheapest threshold was 0.17 for $r=5$ (formula: 0.167), 0.09 for $r=10$ (formula: 0.091) and 0.36 for $r=2$ (formula: 0.333). Using the formula cost less than 0.002 extra per client every time, so the formula works in practice because the probabilities are calibrated.

We looked at the mistakes HistGradientBoosting makes on the test set at $t^* = 0.167$ ($r = 5$). Numbers come from data/processed/error_analysis.json (src/error_analysis.py).

False alarms

39% of false alarms were late in September, against 2% of correctly cleared clients. Because PAY_1 dominates the model, a recent delay alone pushes a client over the low $r=5$ threshold, even when the client then pays in October. They also tend to have low balances relative to their limit (median 0.12), and 19% had no September bill at all. The most confident false alarms ($p \approx 0.78$) were 2-5 months behind in at least three of the six months and still did not default in October.

Missed defaulters

91% of missed defaulters were never late in any of the six months, and only 3% were late in September. Their limits are higher than the defaulters the model catches (median 150,000 vs. 80,000), but they use more of them (median bill / limit 0.55 vs. 0.28 for cleared clients). The three most confident misses ($p \approx 0.03$) had high limits (260k-500k) and paid on time or in full every month. Nothing in six months of billing data separates these clients from good customers, so we see these errors as a limit of the data rather than something more tuning would fix.

Section 11

11. Limitations

About the data

  • No dates: All clients cover the same six months, so we can't do a time-based split (train on earlier, test on later). Our split is random.
  • An unusual period: The data is from Taiwan's 2005-2006 card debt crisis. A 22% default rate is very high, so the model might not work as well in normal times.
  • Credit limit already contains the bank's opinion: The bank set LIMIT_BAL based on how risky it thought the client was, so part of what the model learns from it is the bank's earlier judgement.

About the cost model

  • Same cost for every client: We use one cost ratio $r$ for everybody. In reality, missing a defaulter with a big balance costs more than missing one with a small balance.
  • We can't see what the bank did: The data doesn't say whether the bank contacted or restricted a client, so we can't tell if acting on a flag would actually prevent a default.
